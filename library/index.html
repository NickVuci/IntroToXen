“Gap” is the distance up to the next interval in the set. Gaps under about 30¢ are commas: pairs a temperament might identify.

Row ÷u is the otonal chord 1:3:5… over the undertone u; column ×n is the utonal chord on harmonic n. Every pitch sits at the crossing of one of each, so each cell is a pivot between an otonality and a utonality. Tap a header to hear its chord voiced as true harmonics or subharmonics. Repeated cells (like 3/2 = 9/6) are outlined together when selected.

Harmonics 8–16 (8/8 … 16/8) and subharmonics 8–16 (16/16, 16/15 … 16/8), both read upward from 1/1 like a Scale Workshop scale. Each row is a scale degree with the step that reaches it. Overtone steps shrink as you climb; undertone steps grow. Between neighbouring steps sits the comma n²/(n²−1) that separates them. Tempering one of these commas makes two adjacent steps the same size. Tap a step to hear it, or a comma to hear its two steps one after the other.

Harmonics and subharmonics together

Both 8–16 series combined into one scale: 15 notes, mirror-symmetric around 1/1. Amber brackets mark the three places where an overtone and an undertone nearly collide. Tap a note to hear it against 1/1. The commas that emerge between its intervals are listed in the Commas section.

A comma here is the small difference between two intervals of a scale: the gap a temperament can close. Pick which notes to search, then tap a pair to hear its two intervals, tap the ratio to inspect it below, or find a chord loop (pump) that produces it.

Notes to search
Prime limit

One idea links every pump here: each comma is the difference between two melodic steps that the voice-leading uses as if they were the same. The syntonic comma is 9/8 against 10/9; the augmented diesis is 16/15 against 25/24; marvel is 16/15 against 15/14. Tempering a comma out makes its two steps one step, which is why the loop closes. Pumps that share a step are relatives; pumps with unrelated steps pull the voices in different directions.

Tuning

“Keeps comma” lists the most accurate EDOs in which the comma is still a real step, so the loop drifts on a keyboard much as in just intonation. For the three 5-limit arms that is 53-EDO (81/80 = 1 step, 128/125 = 2, 648/625 = 3); 12-EDO is the familiar tuning that closes all three at once.

A comma pump is a short chord progression that returns to its starting chord by name but not by pitch. Each move keeps common tones, and a held note may change which harmonic it is. The leftover drift is a comma, and every temperament that tempers that comma closes the loop. Play each pump three times in just intonation to hear it slide, then tempered to hear it close. “Closes in” lists the temperaments from the catalog that cover every prime the loop uses and temper its comma.
