How this works

A regular temperament maps just intervals onto a few generators by treating small intervals called commas as unisons. Temper out 81/80 and four fifths make a major third: that's meantone. A linear (rank‑2) temperament has two generators, a period (usually the octave) and a generator, so its mapping says how many of each make every prime.

Define a temperament by its commas or by joining two equal temperaments, then choose how to tune the generator. Stack generators, fold them into the period, and you get scales. A scale with only two step sizes, large (L) and small (s), is a MOS.

Tap or drag across the keys. Each key's width is the size of its step.

On a computer keyboard: home row (A to ') plays degrees 0 to 10, the row above (Q to ]) continues upward, and the bottom row (Z to /) plays the notes below the tonic.

          Getting it onto the HexBoard
          
HexBoard Sync imports Scala files, so the most direct route is: save the Scala tuning, rename it from .scl.txt to .scl, and import it on the Tunings & Layouts page. Then enter the Across and Up-right numbers on the Layout step and include the scale degrees listed above on the Scale & color step.

          
The JSON files hold the same values under the field names of the firmware's preset-sync object model. They are not Sync's own bundle file (hexboard.tuningBundle.v2), whose field layout I couldn't find documented, so Sync may not open them directly.

          
The key map follows the firmware source: 14 rows of 10 keys, odd rows shifted half a key to the right, button 0 at the top left, the center key is button 65, and buttons 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 and 120 are the seven command buttons, so 133 keys play notes; the seven command buttons are left out of the picture. Pitches on the board come from the Across and Up-right numbers.
